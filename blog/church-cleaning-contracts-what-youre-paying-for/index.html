Church Cleaning Contracts: What You're Actually Paying For

The details buried in a cleaning services agreement determine what your congregation actually receives.

Signing a church cleaning contract can feel like a formality, but the details buried in those pages determine what you actually receive — and what you don't. Before a facilities committee signs anything, here's what every church administrator should understand about cleaning service agreements.

The scope of work section is everything

The most important part of any church cleaning contract is the scope of work. This section should detail every task the cleaning crew is expected to perform, how often, and in which areas of your building. Vague language like “general cleaning” is a red flag — look for itemized task lists broken down by space: sanctuary, fellowship hall, nursery, classrooms, restrooms.

Common line items in a church cleaning contract

  • Vacuuming and mopping of all floors (carpet in the sanctuary, tile in the narthex, hardwood on the platform — specify each)
  • Restroom sanitization and restocking (paper, soap, bags)
  • Trash and recycling removal
  • Fellowship hall and kitchen cleaning
  • Pew, chair, and surface wipe-downs
  • Window and glass cleaning (narthex doors, interior partitions)
  • Nursery and classroom sanitizing between services

What's usually not included

Most standard contracts exclude biohazard cleanup, post-event deep cleaning after large gatherings, exterior work, carpet extraction, and pest control. If your building hosts community meals, weddings, or funerals that generate occasional heavy cleaning needs, ask for a supplemental rate schedule upfront.

Watch for these contract clauses

  • Auto-renewal clauses: Many contracts automatically renew for 12 months unless you give 30–60 days' written notice.
  • Price escalation clauses: Some contracts allow annual price increases tied to labor costs.
  • Termination fees: Leaving early may cost you 1–3 months of remaining contract value.
  • Complaint procedures: How do you report a missed clean before Sunday morning? What's the remedy timeline?

Short-term vs. long-term contracts

Month-to-month contracts offer flexibility but typically cost more per visit. Annual contracts lock in pricing and are easier to budget against for a congregation's fiscal year. A trial period is worth asking for if you want to test a new provider before committing long-term.

Always negotiate

Everything is negotiable. Ask about trial periods and performance guarantees. A reputable cleaning company should be willing to customize the contract to your building's actual rhythm — Sunday mornings, midweek programs, and everything in between.
